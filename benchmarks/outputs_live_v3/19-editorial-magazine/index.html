Material Culture // Alentejo, Portugal

THE SILENT QUARRIES

Two hundred meters beneath the sun-bleached olive groves of Vila Viçosa, massive diamond wire saws are slicing monolithic marble monoliths that will anchor the zero-carbon megastructures of the next century.

Words by Helena Vaz de Silva Photography by Tiago Casanova
THE SILENT QUARRIES 43
"We are not digging graves; we are excavating the permanent skeleton of European civil architecture."
— Alvaro Siza, Studio Notes

The descent into the Codaçal rift begins not with darkness, but with an overwhelming, blinding luminescence. In midsummer, the noon sun strikes the sheer 80-meter vertical calcite walls, turning the pit into an immense subterranean cathedral of white dust and mirrored light. Down here, diesel fumes are banned; giant hydraulic wire saws hum with a steady, monastic vibration as diamond beads slice through stone at fourteen meters an hour.

For three decades, architecture treated natural stone as a bourgeois indulgence—a paper-thin cladding glued over steel rebar. Today, structural engineers in Zurich and Lisbon are rediscovering what Roman master builders understood empirically: solid unreinforced post-tensioned stone possesses compressive strength rivaling high-performance concrete, with an embodied carbon footprint reduced by nearly eighty-five percent.
